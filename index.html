From the Travel Desk — My Irish Cousin

Slea Head Drive by Car — Fifty Kilometres of Atlantic, and Every One of Them Earned

The road out of Dingle town narrows at a stone bridge, climbs past a farmhouse, and suddenly the whole Atlantic is in your windscreen. Our correspondent has driven the loop hundreds of times — here is how to do it well: which way round, where to stop, and how to keep your nerve on the narrow bits.

By Shruti, Travel Correspondent

Dingle Peninsula — The full Slea Head loop is only about fifty kilometres, our correspondent writes, but it packs in enough scenery for a whole day — and enough visitors to turn those kilometres into a slow procession if you get the timing wrong. Dingle town is the launch pad: the last place to fill the tank, buy a sandwich and use a proper toilet for the next few hours. Do not skip this step.

Direction matters. Most people, and almost every coach, go clockwise from Dingle town — Ventry first, then Slea Head, then Coumeenoole. Leave Dingle before eight in the morning and you can have the viewpoints to yourself for an hour or two; by ten the coaches arrive, and by eleven the car parks at Coumeenoole are full. If you only remember one thing from the guide, make it that.

As for the stops: Slea Head viewpoint is essential, looking west over the Blasket Islands — the picture everyone expects from this drive. Coumeenoole Beach, below the road and fresh from a starring role in Ryan's Daughter, is the beach everyone photographs. Dunmore Head is the westernmost point of mainland Ireland, and the beehive huts and Famine Cottage reward the historically minded — while Gallarus Oratory, on the north side, deserves its own visit rather than a rushed five minutes.

The narrow bits are simple if you keep your speed down: use the passing places, reverse when you are nearer one, and never wave the sheep out of the way aggressively — they live here and you do not. Rain is no reason to cancel; fog is. Time the second half of the loop for the hour before sunset, when the light comes in low from the west, and you will understand why painters keep coming back.
